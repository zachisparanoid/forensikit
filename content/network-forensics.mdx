Network forensics is the capture, recording, and analysis of network traffic and logs to reconstruct what happened across the wire — who talked to whom, when, over which protocols, and often _what_ was actually transferred. Unlike disk forensics, where the evidence sits still and waits, network evidence is fleeting: if you weren't recording, the packets are gone.

## Two ways to see the network

There are two complementary vantage points, and serious investigations use both.

**Full packet capture (PCAP)** records every byte of every packet — headers _and_ payloads. It is the highest-fidelity source: you can reconstruct entire conversations, extract transferred files, and read cleartext credentials. The cost is volume; capturing a busy link produces enormous files, so full capture is usually targeted or kept in short rolling windows.

**Flow and log analysis** records _metadata about_ connections rather than their contents — source and destination IPs and ports, protocol, byte and packet counts, timestamps. **NetFlow**, IPFIX, and firewall/proxy/DNS logs fall here. Flow data is tiny by comparison, so organizations retain it far longer. You lose payload contents but keep the who/when/how-much, which is often enough to detect exfiltration, beaconing, or lateral movement and to scope an incident.

<Callout type="note" title="Flow data scales; packet capture goes deep">
  A useful mental model: flow/log data is the phone bill (who called whom, when,
  for how long) and full PCAP is the wiretap (the actual conversation). You can
  afford to keep phone bills for months and wiretaps only briefly — so
  investigations often start in flow/logs to find *where* to look, then dive
  into any PCAP that covers that window.
</Callout>

## PCAP basics

A PCAP file is an ordered list of captured packets, each stamped with a capture time and the raw bytes seen on the wire. Tools like Wireshark (GUI) and tshark/tcpdump (command line) parse those bytes layer by layer — Ethernet, then IP, then TCP/UDP, then the application protocol — so you can filter, follow a single TCP stream, and export objects.

```bash
# Capture from an interface to a file (do this only on networks you own/are authorized to monitor)
sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -w capture.pcap

# Read it back and filter for plaintext HTTP
tshark -r capture.pcap -Y http

# Pull out DNS queries to see what names a host resolved
tshark -r capture.pcap -Y dns -T fields -e ip.src -e dns.qry.name

# Follow a single TCP conversation by stream index
tshark -r capture.pcap -q -z follow,tcp,ascii,0
```

To dissect raw captured bytes or extracted payloads outside a packet tool, ForensiKit's [Hex Inspector](/tools/hex-inspector) lets you read structures by offset, and the [String Extractor](/tools/string-extractor) surfaces readable text — handy for spotting hostnames, user agents, or credentials inside a payload. Any file you carve out of a stream can be type-checked with the [File Signature Identifier](/tools/file-signature-id), much like in [File Carving & Signatures](/learn/file-carving-and-signatures).

## Protocols worth knowing

Most analysis comes down to reading a handful of protocols fluently:

- **DNS** — name resolution. Reveals which domains a host contacted, and is a common covert channel (DNS tunneling) for exfiltration.
- **HTTP** — web traffic in cleartext: full URLs, headers, cookies, posted form data, and downloaded files are all readable.
- **TLS/HTTPS** — encrypted web traffic. You can't read the payload, but the handshake still leaks the destination (SNI), certificate details, and timing.
- **SMTP/IMAP/POP3** — email. Cleartext variants expose entire messages and attachments.
- **SMB** — Windows file sharing, central to lateral movement and ransomware spread.
- **DHCP/ARP** — tie IP addresses to specific machines (MAC addresses) at a given time, which is essential for attribution.

## What you can reconstruct

From full packet capture, an analyst can rebuild a great deal:

- **Sessions and timelines** — follow each TCP/UDP conversation end to end and order events precisely.
- **Transferred files** — carve images, documents, and malware straight out of HTTP, SMB, or FTP streams ("file/object extraction").
- **Credentials over cleartext** — Basic-auth headers, HTTP form posts, and plaintext mail logins expose usernames and passwords directly. This is a vivid reminder of why unencrypted protocols are dangerous.
- **Indicators of compromise** — repeated beaconing to a remote IP, unusual ports, or large outbound transfers signaling exfiltration.

  type="warning"
  title="Capturing traffic is interception — get authorization"
>
  Recording network traffic means reading other people's communications, which
  is legally regulated in most jurisdictions. Only capture on networks you own
  or are explicitly authorized to monitor, mind privacy and wiretap law, and
  document the scope. ForensiKit's tools are for analyzing captures you are
  entitled to examine — lab traffic, your own network, or CTF data.
</Callout>

## IDS and NetFlow in practice

Two technologies do the heavy lifting for continuous monitoring:

- **Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS)** like Snort, Suricata, and Zeek (formerly Bro) watch live traffic and flag known-bad signatures or anomalous behavior. Zeek in particular turns raw traffic into rich, queryable logs (connections, DNS, HTTP, TLS, files) that are excellent forensic evidence even when no alert fired.
- **NetFlow/IPFIX collectors** aggregate flow records across the network, giving long-retention visibility into who connected to whom and how much data moved — ideal for spotting slow exfiltration and mapping an intruder's path after the fact.

In a real incident, these are usually where the investigation starts: an IDS alert or an odd flow points you at a host and a time window, and only then do you reach for any PCAP covering it.

## The encryption challenge

The biggest shift in network forensics is pervasive encryption. With most web and app traffic now TLS-protected, payloads are opaque on the wire. That doesn't make the network useless — it changes _what_ you read:

- **Metadata still flows.** Destinations (via SNI, though encrypted SNI is spreading), certificate fingerprints, packet sizes, and timing patterns reveal a lot. **JA3/JA4** fingerprinting can identify the client software from how it negotiates TLS — sometimes spotting malware by its handshake alone.
- **Endpoints become decisive.** When you can't read traffic in flight, evidence shifts to the hosts: the decrypted data lives in their memory and on their disks. This is where network forensics hands off to [Memory Forensics](/learn/memory-forensics), where TLS keys and decrypted buffers can still be recovered.
- **Lawful decryption.** In authorized settings, traffic may be decrypted with logged session keys (an `SSLKEYLOGFILE`) or an enterprise inspection proxy — never by breaking the cryptography.

<Callout type="tip" title="Let metadata point you to the host">
  When payloads are encrypted, treat the network layer as a map, not a
  transcript. Flow records and TLS metadata tell you *which* host to examine and
  *when* — then pivot to that endpoint's memory and disk for the cleartext. The
  wire narrows the search; the host closes the case.
</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Network evidence is ephemeral — without recording in advance, the packets are gone; full PCAP gives depth, flow/logs give scale and retention.
- PCAP analysis (Wireshark, tshark, tcpdump) reconstructs sessions, extracts transferred files, and reads cleartext credentials.
- A handful of protocols — DNS, HTTP, TLS, SMB, DHCP/ARP — carry most of the investigative value, including IP-to-machine attribution.
- IDS (Snort/Suricata/Zeek) and NetFlow/IPFIX provide continuous visibility and are usually where an investigation begins.
- Encryption hides payloads but not metadata; SNI, certificates, sizes, timing, and JA3/JA4 fingerprints still reveal a great deal.
- When traffic is encrypted, pivot to the endpoints — memory and disk hold the decrypted data — and only capture traffic you are authorized to monitor.

</KeyTakeaways>
